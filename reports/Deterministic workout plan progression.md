# Freeze the skeleton, progress the dose

A high-quality deterministic 4-week consumer workout-plan generator should **keep split, rest-day pattern, primary exercises, and warm-up structure fixed**, and express week-to-week progression almost entirely through **small, pre-scheduled changes in sets, effort (RIR), and optionally rest**—not through new workouts, new goals, or weekly exercise remixes. That pattern matches both evidence-based coaching (ACSM’s 2026 emphasis on consistency over complex periodization) and the commercial “coach-template” products that map cleanly onto pure functions of onboarding inputs ([ACSM 2026 guidelines announcement](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/); [Peloton Pump Up the Volume](https://www.onepeloton.com/classes/pump-up-the-volume); [Hevy Trainer Settings](https://help.hevyapp.com/hc/en-us/articles/43572343844247-How-Hevy-Trainer-Settings-Work)). Experience should gate **weekly hard sets per muscle, sets per exercise, exercise-pool complexity, and failure proximity**, with true beginners near **~6–10 hard sets/muscle/week, 1–2 sets/exercise, ~8–15 reps, stop ~2–4 RIR**, while a “basic / some experience” tier sits slightly higher (**~8–14 weekly sets, 2–3 sets/exercise, ~8–12 reps, ~1–3 RIR**) without jumping to advanced volume ([Medscape ACSM recommendations table](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/717047_9); [RP Training Volume Landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)). Beginner-friendly warm-ups should be a **5–10 minute Raise → dynamic mobility → optional compound ramp-set** package, preferring easy machines over jumping when available and swapping impact drills for controlled mobility when age, BMI, or injury flags apply ([Nike Warm-Up Training Tips](https://www.nike.com/a/warmup-training-tips); [NSCA Introduction to Dynamic Warm-Up](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/kinetic-select/introduction-to-dynamic-warm-up/)). PacerGo already owns most of the scaffolding—4-week blocks, experience-tiered sets/reps, experience-aware exercise ranking, +1 set in weeks 3–4, and `needsLowImpact` warm-up swaps—so the highest-ROI upgrades are richer week scalars, clearer Beginner vs Basic defaults, machine-aware Raise fallbacks, and double-progression copy rather than a Fitbod-style live recommender.

## Fixed mesocycles beat weekly remixes for pure generators

Successful consumer apps cluster into session-adaptive recommenders (Fitbod), human coach/instructor blocks of about **4–6 weeks** (Ladder, Peloton Strength collections, Caliber plans, modern NTC programs), and journey/AI-coach cycles with assessment and peak weeks (Freeletics). A no-LLM composer maps cleanest onto the middle cluster: **same programmatic skeleton for the block**, with week indices changing load, reps, volume, rest, and/or planned intensity cues ([Peloton Strength+](https://www.onepeloton.com/en-CA/strength-plus-app); [joinladder.com](https://www.joinladder.com/); [Caliber workout app](https://caliberstrong.com/workout-app/)). Fitbod rebuilds each session from recovery windows, estimated strength, and logged history—powerful after the user has data, but **out of scope** for generation-time determinism ([Fitbod algorithm blog](https://fitbod.me/blog/fitbod-algorithm/); [How Fitbod Creates Your Workout](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/360004429814-How-Fitbod-Creates-Your-Workout)). Hevy Trainer is the closest public peer for PacerGo’s stance: programs are built by **algorithm, explicitly not LLM**, with a Program Variety setting whose default “Balanced” refreshes exercises only after about **six weeks**, and “Consistent” keeps the same exercises every week ([Hevy Trainer Explained](https://help.hevyapp.com/hc/en-us/articles/38385724273047-Hevy-Trainer-Explained-How-It-Builds-Your-Workout-Program); [How Hevy Trainer Settings Work](https://help.hevyapp.com/hc/en-us/articles/43572343844247-How-Hevy-Trainer-Settings-Work)). For a four-week PacerGo plan, that implies a default of **Consistent**, not Variable.

Evidence and coach education push the same freeze list. ACSM’s 2026 position-stand messaging stresses that the biggest benefits come from **consistency**, that complex periodization did **not consistently** outperform simpler training for average healthy adults, and that programs must stay sustainable enough to maintain ([ACSM 2026 guidelines announcement](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/); [PMC overview of reviews](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)). Open-source `routine-engine` states the engineering reason plainly: rotating exercises every week makes progressive overload **impossible to see**, so exercises should hold for a **four-week block** ([sugarshaneaz/routine-engine](https://github.com/sugarshaneaz/routine-engine)). Starting Strength’s novice linear progression keeps an A/B template and nonconsecutive schedule fixed while only loads change ([Starting Strength programs](https://startingstrength.com/article/programs)). Zourdos and colleagues note that periodization model choice is of **little importance in novices**, who should prioritize technique, adherence, and avoiding overtraining ([JSCR modified DUP study](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2016/03000/modified_daily_undulating_periodization_model.24.aspx)). What should change with `week_index` is therefore narrow: load or RIR targets, sets per exercise within a **+0–2** band, and optionally a mild week-4 volume cut for intermediate-plus users—not new splits, drifting rest days, wholesale remixes, or endurance→hypertrophy→strength phase hopping inside one consumer month.

For implementable W1→W4 scalars without logged 1RMs, three schemes dominate. True beginners should use **novice linear progression inside a fixed template**: double-progression (reps then load) or mild RIR tightening, volume flat or only +0–1 set by week 4, **no required deload**—RP notes beginners can often train for many weeks before systemic MRV, while hard deloads every 3–4 weeks matter more for advanced trainees ([RP back hypertrophy / periodization notes](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/back-hypertrophy-training-tips); [ACSM 2–10% / 2-for-2 progression](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/717047_9)). A “basic / experienced” hypertrophy path can start near MEV and add about **1–2 hard sets/week** through weeks 3–4, optionally holding or cutting volume ~40–50% in week 4 if the product treats the user as intermediate-plus ([RP Training Volume Landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)). When percent-1RM is unknown—the consumer default—**mild intensity ramps via RIR** (e.g. ~3 → ~1–2 across the block) are safer than inventing weekly %1RM jumps. Freeletics-style **complexity ladders** (bodyweight squat → more reps/shorter rest → jump variant → split squat) are fully deterministic if keyed off week and experience, but Freeletics itself often gates unlocks on performance, which is live feedback rather than generation-time logic ([Best App for Progressive Overload at Home](https://www.freeletics.com/en/progressive-overload-at-home/)). Within one experience tier and one four-week plan, the safer rule is still to **hold the chosen ladder rung** and progress load/reps; climb machine→barbell between plans, not inside every week ([OpenExamPrep / ACE CPT movement progressions](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/ace-cpt/exercise-selection/movement-progressions-regressions); [ACSM 2026 machines vs free weights](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/)).

## Beginner and basic differ by weekly dose and complexity, not by reinventing the split each week

Public apps rarely publish a “year 0 vs year 1” table; they use coarse Beginner / Intermediate / Advanced tiers that mainly gate **exercise complexity**, recovery aggressiveness, and set/rep calibration ([Fitbod Fitness Experience](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/29976088485143-Fitness-Experience); [Hevy Trainer Settings](https://help.hevyapp.com/hc/en-us/articles/43572343844247-How-Hevy-Trainer-Settings-Work)). Where concrete numbers appear, beginners cluster around **~3×8–10**, conservative starts, and form over failure: Nike’s fundamentals advice starts new lifters at **3 sets of 10** in week 1; Caliber’s beginner blueprint keeps most lifts at **3×8–10** with last-rep difficulty but not failure, progressing by +1 rep or small load while holding the routine for weeks ([Nike 5 Tips for Smarter Strength Training](https://www.nike.com/gb/a/smarter-strength-training-fundamentals); [Caliber A Great Beginner’s Workout Routine](https://caliberstrong.com/blog/great-beginners-workout-routine/)). Fitbod’s beginner guidance emphasizes form and avoiding early failure; experienced users may use Max Effort / failure more ([Getting Started with Fitbod](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/30721771750039-Getting-Started-with-Fitbod-A-New-User-s-Guide)). Freeletics beginner journeys favor technically easier movements and, on some paths, higher-rep easier work ([Bodyweight Training Journeys](https://help.freeletics.com/hc/en-us/articles/360008600540-Bodyweight-Training-Journeys)).

Position stands supply the numeric backbone apps imply but seldom publish. ACSM 2009 still frames novice strength work as roughly **60–70% 1RM for 8–12 reps**, **1–3 sets per exercise**, full-body **2–3 days/week**, with hypertrophy for novice/intermediate around **70–85% 1RM, 8–12 reps, 1–3 sets**, and frequency rising toward **3–4** then **4–6** days as status advances ([Medscape ACSM recommendations table](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/717047_9)). ACSM 2026’s practical hypertrophy target for general adults is about **~10 weekly sets per muscle group**, with diminishing returns beyond roughly **2–3 sets/exercise** for strength and ~**18–20 weekly sets** for hypertrophy maximizers—supporting consumer caps well below “hardcore” **30–40** set programs ([ACSM 2026 guidelines announcement](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/); [Stronger by Science volume](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/volume/)). NSCA frequency guidance locks novices into **2–3 nonconsecutive full-body days**, intermediates into **3–4** days often via upper/lower or push/pull, and warns that trained clients **cannot progress** on only 1–2 days/week (maintenance only) ([NSCA frequency article](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/kinetic-select/determination-of-resistance-training-frequency/)). RP landmarks put maintenance volume near **~6 sets/muscle/week**, with beginners growing easily near that floor and MAV rising with advancement ([RP Training Volume Landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)). NASM Phase 1 (stabilization) for true novices uses **12–20 reps, 1–3 sets, 50–70% 1RM** over blocks of about **4–6 weeks**—a useful “technique-first” alternate to 8–12 when form is fragile ([NASM OPT model](https://www.nasm.org/certified-personal-trainer/the-opt-model); [NASM Phase 1 blog](https://blog.nasm.org/nasm-optimum-performance-training)).

Mapped onto PacerGo’s onboarding language, **true beginner / no experience** should mean fewer technical variants, **2–3 full-body days**, **1–2 sets per exercise**, weekly hard sets near **MV–MEV (~6–10)**, reps **8–12 or 12–15**, stop short of failure. **Basic / some gym experience (&lt;1 year)** should keep the same compounds visible across weeks but allow **2–3 sets**, weekly hard sets nearer **~8–14**, optional upper/lower at 3–4 days, and slightly harder unilateral or free-weight rungs—not Olympic lifts or advanced skill work. Hevy’s frequency→split defaults (1–3 days full body, 4 upper/lower, 5–6 PPL variants) are a ready deterministic table for that second lever ([Hevy Trainer Settings](https://help.hevyapp.com/hc/en-us/articles/43572343844247-How-Hevy-Trainer-Settings-Work)). When frequency or session minutes change, the safe coupling rule is to fix **weekly sets per muscle** from goal × experience, then redistribute across exposures—**never** multiply per-session volume by extra days, and raise only one major stressor at a time (volume **or** intensity **or** frequency) ([Stronger by Science volume](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/volume/); [BJSM 2023 network meta-analysis](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/early/2023/07/06/bjsports-2023-106807.full.pdf?ijkey=t4XLijcgg5w3fvM&keytype=ref); [NSCA frequency article](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/kinetic-select/determination-of-resistance-training-frequency/)). User-facing progression copy should teach **double progression** inside a fixed range (“add load when you hit the top of 8–10”) even when the app cannot auto-update loads—Caliber and logger apps rely on that instruction while the template stays fixed ([Caliber beginner routine](https://caliberstrong.com/blog/great-beginners-workout-routine/); [Cleveland Clinic progressive overload](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/progressive-overload)).

## Warm-ups should raise temperature, then rehearse the day’s patterns

Guidelines and consumer products converge on a two-layer warm-up: a short general **Raise** of temperature and heart rate, then **movement-specific dynamic mobility/activation**, with progressive warm-up sets on the first heavy compound when loads are used. ACSM-associated teaching and NSCA materials prescribe about **5–10 minutes** of light-to-moderate general work, prefer dynamic over pre-session static stretching for resistance performance, and follow with specific drills similar to the upcoming lifts ([UH Libraries warm-up chapter](https://uhlibraries.pressbooks.pub/appliedexercise/chapter/warm-up-for-different-exercise-types/); [NSCA Introduction to Dynamic Warm-Up](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/kinetic-select/introduction-to-dynamic-warm-up/); [ACSM Quantity and Quality of Exercise PDF](https://www.abom.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Quantity_and_Quality_of_Exercise_for_Developing.26-002.pdf)). ACE’s practical heuristic budgets warm-up at roughly **12–20% of session time**—about **8–12 minutes** inside a 60-minute session—which for PacerGo’s 30–60 minute consumer range translates cleanly to **~5–6 min / ~6–8 min / ~8–10 min** as session length grows ([ACE activity-specific warm-up](https://www.acefitness.org/certifiednewsarticle/2910/a-detailed-guide-to-designing-activity-specific-warm-up-and-mobility-drills/)). Athletic RAMP protocols of **10–30 minutes** are optional for sport and usually too long for MVP 30-minute plans ([Jeffreys RAMP PDF](https://www.scottishathletics.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/Warm-up-revisted-.pdf)).

Nike’s PT-backed consumer recipe is the best portable default when equipment is uneven: **1–2 minutes** light walk/jog, then squat-to-stands, World’s Greatest Stretch, leg swings, inchworms, glute bridges, with plyometric hops reserved mainly for HIIT/power rather than general strength ([Nike Warm-Up Training Tips](https://www.nike.com/a/warmup-training-tips); [Nike Workout for Beginners](https://www.nike.com/gb/a/workout-for-beginner)). Caliber’s beginner blog pairs **5 minutes light cardio** with **three ramp sets** on the first exercise (very light ×12 → ~50% ×10 → ~70% ×4) ([Caliber beginner routine](https://caliberstrong.com/blog/great-beginners-workout-routine/)). Fitbod separates optional mobility warm-ups from **Warm-Up Sets** that only appear when working weight leaves room for a useful ramp, and skips those ramps for bodyweight/band/accessories ([Fitbod Warm-Up Sets](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/360006337634-Warm-Up-Sets); [Fitbod How to start strength training](https://fitbod.me/blog/how-to-start-strength-training/)). Experience should change **complexity and intensity of the warm-up**, not whether one exists: beginners keep Raise shorter/easier and favor low-skill drills; experienced users can shorten Raise slightly and emphasize specific drills plus progressive sets ([NSCA Basics of Strength and Conditioning Manual](https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/48a12160221541acbdc048498d77192d/basics_of_strength_and_conditioning_manual.pdf)).

When impact should stay low—PacerGo’s existing triggers of injury obstacle, age ≥50, or BMI ≥30—guidelines and kinesiology advice prefer **walking and low-impact machines** over running/jumping, and NIA-style strength prep uses the exercises with **less weight** rather than plyometric primers ([GMU exercise tips for obese individuals](https://cehd.gmu.edu/features/2024/02/23/exercise-tips-for-reducing-risk-of-injury-in-obese-individuals/); [National Institute on Aging – Three types of exercise](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/exercise-and-physical-activity/three-types-exercise-can-improve-your-health-and-physical)). Machine Raise preference for MVP is pragmatic: **stationary bike → elliptical → easy row → treadmill walk**, then low-impact bodyweight marching when machines are absent; jumping jacks / high knees only for healthy non-low-impact users without machines, kept short, and **never** as the whole warm-up ([NSCA Introduction to Dynamic Warm-Up](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/kinetic-select/introduction-to-dynamic-warm-up/); [Verywell Health bike vs treadmill](https://www.verywellhealth.com/exercise-bike-vs-treadmill-11733750)). Always finish Raise with the same small dynamic set (arm circles, leg swings, bodyweight squat, plus session-specific activation when catalog allows). Missing machines must **fall through**, not omit the warm-up—the Fitbod pattern of omitting foam-roll soft tissue when equipment is absent without blocking the workout is the right product instinct ([Fitbod Stretching / Warm-up & Cool-down](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/360019715433-Stretching-Warm-up-Cool-down)).

## PacerGo already owns the rails; upgrade the week scalars and warm-up Raise chain

PacerGo’s shared generator is already a coach-template mesocycle: **`WEEKS_PER_PLAN = 4`**, stable focus sequences per split, experience-gated pools via `experienceFit` / `compareForLevel` / `suitsExperience`, and `SETS_REPS_BY_EXPERIENCE` defaults (`no_experience` and `beginner` at **2 sets / 12–15**, `intermediate` **3 / 10–12**, `advanced` **4 / 8–10**). Week-to-week progression today is essentially **`progressionSets`**: +1 set on main lifts in weeks 3–4 unless lack-of-time or injuries obstacles apply. That is the right *kind* of lever—volume scalar on a frozen skeleton—but research supports enriching it into an explicit table: true beginners mostly **flat volume + RIR/copy guidance**, basic users **+1 set mid-block**, intermediate-plus optional mild week-4 unload; emit double-progression instructions rather than pretending loads auto-update without logging. Split recommendation already maps novice status toward simpler full-body templates and Hevy-like frequency logic; the reuse win is to treat **weekly sets per muscle** as the primary dose when days or duration change, redistributing instead of cloning per-session volume.

On warm-ups, PacerGo already encodes **`needsLowImpact`** and swaps `jumping-jack` / `high-knees` for `arm-circles`, `leg-swings-stretch`, and `bodyweight-squat`—aligned with the literature’s low-impact package and requiring **no new onboarding question**. The main gap versus Nike/Caliber/NSCA is the missing **machine-aware Raise** layer and short compound ramp sets for weighted sessions: prefer easy `cycling_stationary` / `elliptical` / `rowing` / treadmill walk when those equipment ids exist, keep jumping only as a short no-machine fallback for non-low-impact users, and add catalog slugs for march-in-place / step-touch so low-impact home users still get a true Raise. Experience overlays can reuse the existing enum—beginners prefer machine or march paths and more coaching-style mobility; intermediates may shorten Raise by about a minute in 30-minute sessions. Cooldowns already lean on real stretches from `STRETCH_LIBRARY`; warm-up should stay dynamic-first, with optional gentle static only for known stiff areas in older/low-impact users, not as the default for everyone ([ACSM Quantity and Quality of Exercise PDF](https://www.abom.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Quantity_and_Quality_of_Exercise_for_Developing.26-002.pdf)).

What PacerGo should **not** copy for generation-time logic is Fitbod’s continuous ML recommender, Freeletics Hell Week extremes for absolute beginners, RiR/Max Effort AMRAP autoregulation, wearable recovery percentages, or human coach exercise flags—those are **live feedback** loops after logging ([Fitbod algorithm blog](https://fitbod.me/blog/fitbod-algorithm/); [Freeletics Training Journeys update](https://www.freeletics.com/en/blog/posts/update-freeletics-training-journeys/); [Future Pro workout FAQ](https://faq.future.co/en/articles/12073331-what-should-i-expect-from-a-future-pro-workout)). Product narrative should follow Hevy’s transparency—**rule-based progressive plans**—rather than “AI coach” branding when the composer is deterministic. Exact week-by-week set tables used inside Ladder or Peloton instructor programs remain non-public; PacerGo must own explicit default tables informed by ACSM/RP/app patterns, not reverse-engineered proprietary schedules.

## Conclusion

The strategic shift is from “make week 3 feel different by changing the workout” to “make week 3 feel progressive by changing the dose on a workout the user already recognizes.” That single design choice reconciles ACSM’s consistency doctrine, novice periodization evidence, and the commercial products whose UX users already trust for multi-week strength blocks. PacerGo’s next quality jump is therefore less about inventing a new periodization philosophy and more about publishing deterministic tables—experience × weekly sets, week-index scalars, Raise fallback chains, and double-progression copy—on top of rails that already freeze the skeleton and gate exercise difficulty by experience.
